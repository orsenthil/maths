A predict-and-verify math puzzle

The Chocolate Snap

Based on Breaking Chocolate Bars, Cut the Knot.

How to play

  1. Set the bar's rows and columns.
  2. Predict how many breaks it'll take to reduce it to individual squares.
  3. Break it however you like — click a piece, then click exactly where to snap it.
  4. See how close your guess was, and notice something suspicious about the number.

Why does it always come out the same?

Every single break turns one piece into two — no exceptions, no matter which piece you choose or where along it you snap. So each break adds exactly one piece to the table. A whole bar starts as 1 piece and has to end as rows × columns separate unit squares, so the number of breaks is forced to be exactly rows × columns − 1. Always.

You can also see it by splitting the bar in two: if a piece of size m needs m − 1 breaks (true for a single square, where it needs 0), then a bar split into two pieces of size m1 and m2 needs (m1 − 1) + (m2 − 1) breaks for the pieces, plus the 1 break that made the split — which totals (m1 + m2) − 1, the same formula again, one size up. That's induction: it holds for 1, so it holds for everything built out of 1s.

Notice it doesn't even matter whether you get there with a 3×4 bar, a 2×6 bar, or a 1×12 strip — only the total number of squares matters. And it's also why "whoever makes the last break wins" wouldn't really be a game: the total number of moves is fixed the moment the bar is chosen, so the winner comes down to who moves first, not anything either player decides along the way.

Reference: A. Bogomolny, Breaking Chocolate Bars, Cut the Knot.
